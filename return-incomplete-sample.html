Return Incomplete

A. J. Malan
Chapter One

Recovery

Monday · 10 February 2364 · 05:41 HRS GMT
Lower Clapton · London · 51.56N / 0.06W

The first impact came through the wall behind his bed as a dull thump. The second brought a woman's voice, sharpened by chemicals and lack of sleep. By the third, Rook was upright with one hand on the floor before the rest of him had agreed he was awake.

He stopped there.

There was no weapon under the bed. There had not been for six years. His hand had still gone to the place where one ought to have been. Outside, Kelly Price shouted something about a stolen purse. A man's voice answered from farther down the corridor, laughing in the wrong place. Jaz Turner. Rook checked the time on the wall display. 05:41. Rain whispered against the window with the soft persistence London had spent two centuries failing to improve. He sat for another second, listening.

Kelly hit the opposite door again. The old composite panel flexed in its frame. Whoever lived behind it did not answer. Sensible. The building's security service had been reduced to remote response during the last contract review, and remote response meant a polite voice would eventually ask everybody to stop doing whatever they had already finished doing. Rook stood. His left foot found the floor correctly. The right followed half a beat late, an error small enough that nobody else would have noticed it and familiar enough that he did not dignify it by waiting. He crossed the studio, pulled on yesterday's trousers and opened his door.

Kelly had both hands against the flat opposite and was drawing one boot back for another kick. She wore leggings, a man's raincoat and no shoes suitable for the weather. Her hair had been cut badly on one side and not at all on the other. The pupils that turned toward Rook were too large for the corridor lights. Jaz was sitting on the floor beside the dead lift with a rectangular medical case balanced across his knees. He looked delighted to see him. "Morning, Tom," Jaz said. His voice carried the bright, careful friendliness of a man who had discovered that the world was full of potential investors. "You in the market for a dermal printer?" Rook looked at the case. ST JOSEPH'S COMMUNITY CLINIC was printed across the lid beneath a red inventory strip that had been partly scraped away. "No." Jaz ran one palm over the case. "Proper one. Does grafts, burn patches, little stuff. Barely used." Kelly turned back to the opposite door. "She's got my purse." Rook looked at the splintered edge where her boot had begun to separate the outer laminate. "No, she hasn't." Kelly's head came around. "How the fuck would you know?" "Because you sold it to Marty on Tuesday."

For a moment, her face became very still. Rook had learned to watch that moment with Kelly. Anger was noisy. The pause before it decided where to land was more useful. Jaz laughed again. "Told you." Kelly swung toward him instead. "You shut your mouth. You were there." "I was asleep," Jaz said. "You were fucking not," Kelly snapped. Jaz lifted the medical case as if it might serve as evidence of a busy professional morning. "I've got appointments." Kelly crossed the corridor in two steps and kicked his shin. Jaz swore, dropped the case and came up off the floor much faster than his posture had suggested he could. For two seconds they were chest to chest, both talking, neither listening. Rook stepped between them before Jaz's right hand finished closing. He did not shove him. He put two fingers against the inside of Jaz's wrist and moved the hand down. Jaz looked at the fingers, then at Rook. "Not in the corridor," Rook said.

Jaz's mouth worked once. He let the hand open. Kelly gave Rook a look containing several forms of accusation, none of which she appeared willing to test. "Nothing to do with you, Tom." Rook glanced at the damaged door opposite. "It is at six in the morning." That seemed to offend her more than moral judgement would have. Kelly muttered something about everybody thinking they were better than everybody else and stalked toward the stairs. She shouldered the fire door hard enough to make its closer complain. Jaz watched her go, rubbing his shin. "She's a nightmare." Rook looked at him. Jaz considered the information in Rook's face and bent to retrieve the clinic case. "So that's a no on the printer." "Strong no," Rook said. "Know anyone?" Jaz asked.

Rook went back inside and shut the door. His flat was one room pretending to be four: narrow kitchenette, badly folding bed, smoked-glass bathroom partition. The only generous thing was the window. It looked south-west over Lower Clapton and the long wet confusion of London beyond it. New construction rose wherever money had decided ground level was unreliable. Old brick terraces sat between elevated service lines and deep rain gardens. Farther south, expensive glass caught the first grey of morning while roofs, rail and towers disappeared into rain. Rook put water on for coffee.

The wall display had accumulated three messages overnight. Two were automated. One wanted him to renew the building's enhanced security subscription despite the fact that enhanced security had not prevented somebody stealing a clinic dermal printer. The other reminded him that his neural interface insurer required a quarterly diagnostic within eleven days. The third was from Emma Clarke. He opened it.

YOU WORKING TODAY?

There was no greeting because Emma knew what time he read messages and did not pretend either of them wanted conversation before caffeine. Rook typed YES. Her reply arrived before the kettle boiled.

GOOD. SILVERTOWN. EASY ONE. ACTIVE INDUSTRIAL FRAME, INSOLVENT CONTRACTOR, LENDER WARRANT. SITE REP MEETS 0730. DON'T BREAK IT.

A second message followed.

AND USE THE PROPER KIT.

Rook drank half the coffee standing at the counter. He did not answer the second message. Before leaving, Rook rinsed the cup and set it upside down beside the sink. Three old image plates stood on the narrow shelf beside the window. They were physical things, thin enough to flex and old enough that one had lost some of its depth. Rook had never bothered replacing it. One showed a group of people arranged badly enough that nobody could have mistaken it for an official photograph. A soft knock sounded against the glass. The grey-and-white cat sat on the outside sill. Rook looked at it. The cat looked back. “You’re early.” He opened the narrow side pane and set a shallow dish on the exterior ledge. The cat retreated two careful steps while he emptied food into it, then returned as soon as his hand was inside again. Rook closed the pane.

It wasn’t his cat.

By 06:20 the rain had thickened from weather into infrastructure. Rook left the block through a lobby that smelled of wet clothing, old cooking oil and the citrus disinfectant used by the municipal cleaning drones. One of the drones was stationary beneath the mail wall with its service panel open. Somebody had removed the battery during the night. Outside, London was already working. Delivery frames moved beneath the covered lane on rubberised feet. A council maintenance body stood knee-deep in an opened drain, pulling plastic mesh from the intake. Two schoolchildren followed route guidance projected onto the wet pavement. An old woman ignored it and crossed where she always crossed. Rook joined the flow toward the station.

People still called it the Overground although most of the line through the eastern districts had been rebuilt twice and raised once. The name had survived because London preferred historical inaccuracy to administrative clarity. His right hand trembled while he waited at the gate. Not much: thumb against forefinger, a fine movement made visible only because he had nothing in it. He closed the hand, opened it, then rested it against his coat pocket. The tremor stopped when given a job. The gate read his fare authority and flashed amber.

ADAPTIVE ACCESS ROUTE AVAILABLE.

He dismissed it. The platform lift was thirty metres away; the stairs were twelve. He took the stairs. By the time the train came, the muscles behind his right knee had begun the small tight complaint that meant they would hurt later. Rook filed the information with the rest. Pain was useful when it remained specific. The carriage windows turned the wet city into long grey bands as they headed south. Headlines floated silently above the doors: lunar talks, Titanian core allocations, another parliamentary inquiry. Rook let them pass. At Canning Town, half the carriage emptied into the orbital transfer concourse. He continued east toward Silvertown. Emma called as he stepped off the train.

Her face appeared in the corner of his vision when he accepted, dark hair tied back, unnecessary reading glasses in place. Emma disliked menus projected directly onto her retinas. Rook had never understood why, but respected the consistency. "Hargreaves is already there," she said. Rook descended the station steps into rain that had found a way under the canopy. "He's early." "He's billing by the hour," Emma replied. She answered somebody off-screen, then returned. "Target's a Briggs Twelve heavy utility. Thames Civic Maintenance had it on a long lease through Harcourt. Company went under six weeks ago. Lender wants it back before the liquidator decides it belongs to somebody else." "Operator?" Rook asked. Emma's eyes shifted to another display. "David Malik. Fifty-nine. Site plant supervisor. He's been running the Twelve since it was new. Lease ended with the company. He was served notice twice." "Active now?" Rook asked. "Has been since four this morning," Emma replied. "Harcourt tried the standard compliance lock. Malik pulled the external network before it completed." Rook stepped around a courier who had stopped dead beneath the shelter to argue with a navigation prompt. "Then it's not easy." Emma smiled without warmth. "Everything is easy from my desk." "Proper kit?" Her expression sharpened. "Yes. The words have kept their meaning since I sent them."

Rook ended the call before she could improve on the point. The old Silvertown service district sat behind a flood channel and infrastructure rebuilt without quite replacing what came before. Brick warehouses survived beneath flood skins and freightways. The Thames was hidden behind defence walls, but its presence lived in every ramp, pump housing and yellow insurance line. Thames Civic Maintenance occupied a fenced yard between a drainage works and an automated fabrication plant. The company name was still fixed above the gate, though somebody had removed the illuminated CIVIC from the sign and left THAMES MAINTENANCE to weather alone. Chris Hargreaves waited beneath the gate canopy in a clean navy coat and shoes built for rooms where surfaces stayed dry.

He held out a hand. "Thomas Rook? Chris Hargreaves, Harcourt Leasing." Rook shook it. Hargreaves's grip was brief and professional. His eyes moved to the interface scars disappearing beneath Rook's collar, then away again quickly enough to count as manners. "Where is it?" Rook asked. Hargreaves turned toward the yard. "Bay Four. Malik's inside it." "Biological body on site?" Rook asked. "Office block. First floor. He has an old local couch in the supervisor's room," Hargreaves replied. He started walking and Rook fell beside him. "We have lawful recovery authority, full physical access and a remote-disconnect instruction. I can forward the warrant again if you need it." "I read it," Rook said. Hargreaves looked relieved, as though this distinguished Rook from previous contractors. "Good. I'd prefer not to escalate. David's angry, but he isn't violent." "People don't have to be violent for a two-tonne frame to kill you." Hargreaves glanced at him. "Two point four." Rook looked across the yard toward Bay Four. "Better."

Rain rattled against the roofs. Most of the yard's bays were empty now, with tool shadows on the walls and painted rectangles where charging cradles had stood. A row of inactive worker frames waited beneath a lean-to, stripped of batteries and valuable control modules. Bay Four remained powered.

The Briggs Twelve stood beneath the overhead crane with its back to the open doors. It was not elegant. Industrial Frames rarely were. It stood a little over two and a half metres high, broad through the torso, with two primary arms ending in five-fingered hands large enough to wrap around a man's chest. Its short legs and wide feet had been built for wet concrete, broken ground and people who treated maintenance intervals as suggestions. Seventeen years had made it personal. The left shoulder casing was a different shade of yellow. One knee carried a mismatched repair plate. Somebody had fitted textured rubber inside the right forearm where no machine required grip. A small blue Arsenal cannon sat beneath one sensor housing. Rook stopped at the threshold. The frame turned its head toward him.

"You the recovery man?" Malik's voice came through the external speaker. "Yes," Rook answered. "Then recover yourself somewhere else," Malik said. Hargreaves exhaled quietly beside Rook. "David, we've done this." The Briggs raised one hand. The gesture was unmistakably human despite the weight behind it. "Chris, I have spent six weeks doing this with people who send emails and then go home. You don't get to say we've done it." Rook watched the frame rather than the argument. Malik had positioned it well, between two floor rails and close enough to the crane column that nobody could reach the rear service panel without entering the left arm's reach. The external network lead had been cut. The spine battery showed eighty-one per cent charge. Hours. The bay doors were open behind Rook, but Malik had no good route out. A height bar across the yard entrance would stop the Briggs unless he tore it down, and the frame was valuable enough that Malik had probably spent the morning calculating exactly how not to damage it. Angry and prepared, but not irrational. That was easier. "Mr Malik," Rook said, "you know the warrant's valid." The optical cluster held on him. "You a solicitor as well?" "No," Rook said. "Then don't explain my rights to me," Malik replied. "Wasn't going to," Rook said.

That produced a pause. Hargreaves shifted his weight beside Rook. He had placed himself half a metre too far into the bay. If Malik moved suddenly, Hargreaves would retreat straight into the door track and lose a second discovering it was there. Rook moved one step to the side. Hargreaves followed without noticing why. "They owe me eleven weeks," Malik said through the frame. "Company folds, directors disappear, pension administrator says the account is under review, but Harcourt gets its machine back on a Sunday morning. Funny what still works." "It's Monday," Hargreaves said. Rook looked at him. Hargreaves rubbed his mouth. "Sorry." The Briggs's head angled a fraction. Rook could not see Malik's face, but he could imagine the expression. "I offered to buy it," Malik continued. "Harcourt rejected the bid." Hargreaves answered carefully. "The valuation is higher than your offer." "The valuation was done by a model that thinks the left hip is original." Rook looked at the hip. It wasn't. "How long have you been primary operator?" he asked. "Seventeen years," Malik said. "Anyone else calibrated?" Rook asked. The frame's shoulders changed by less than a centimetre. Pride. "Not properly."

Rook believed him. Long-term industrial bodies accumulated their operators in small ways. Control software adapted, but so did metal. Replacement parts were fitted around habits. Limits were learned rather than read. A man stopped thinking about the half-degree delay in a shoulder because his brain had already corrected before intention reached it. After enough years, the distinction between machine accommodation and human accommodation became academic. Rook knew something about that. He kept the thought where it belonged.

"I'm taking the frame today," he said. The right hand of the Briggs closed once, metal fingers meeting with a soft hydraulic knock. "Then you'll cut me out of it." Hargreaves turned toward Rook. "The warrant permits forced disconnect after a refusal." Rook had read that too. The clause sat on page nine beneath NEURAL ASSET POSSESSION, written in the clean language institutions used when they wanted consequences to sound like settings. "His couch certified?" Rook asked. Hargreaves looked toward the office block. "It's registered legacy equipment." "That's not what I asked," Rook said. "No," Hargreaves admitted. "Certification expired." Rook looked back at the Briggs. A forced disconnect through an uncertified couch would probably be fine. Probably meant Malik waking in the supervisor's office with a migraine, vomiting, perhaps losing an hour of proprioception while his biological body remembered being two and a half metres tall. Probably was a word Rook had once heard from doctors standing beside beds. "I'm not hard-cutting him," he said. Hargreaves's patience thinned. "Mr Rook, Harcourt hired your firm because your profile says you specialise in non-compliant recovery." "I do," Rook said. "Then what exactly are we doing?" Hargreaves asked. Rook looked up at the overhead crane, then at the service trench running beneath the frame. "Recovering it."

He walked out of the bay.

Hargreaves caught up three steps later. "Where are you going?" "To see what he forgot." Behind them, Malik said something through the Briggs's speaker that the rain swallowed. The yard's maintenance office had been stripped almost as thoroughly as the bays. Rook found an old site schematic on a wall panel that still accepted local touch after refusing three network requests. The display showed power, drainage, crane rails, fire suppression and the buried service trench beneath Bay Four. The trench had two access points. Malik had parked over one. The other opened in Bay Three. Rook studied the line between them. "There's nothing under there but buswork," Hargreaves said from behind him. "Hydraulic recovery feed," Rook said. "For the bay equipment," Hargreaves replied. "And emergency plant isolation," Rook said. Hargreaves leaned closer to the schematic. "If you isolate Bay Four, he'll switch to internal battery." "Already has," Rook said. "Then I don't understand," Hargreaves replied. "No," Rook said.

Hargreaves looked at him, uncertain whether he had been insulted. Rook left him with the question. Bay Three held an old Briggs service frame in a wall rack, roughly human-shaped so it could use human ladders. One arm ended in a hand, the other in a tool head. Battery: twenty-three per cent. The service log listed bad left-ankle feedback and two overdue neural-interface faults.

Hargreaves read over his shoulder. "That thing hasn't passed inspection in eleven months." "Ten," Rook said. "It says eleven," Hargreaves replied. "Last month doesn't count if nobody turned it on," Rook said. Hargreaves stared at him. "I don't think that's how inspection works." Rook crouched beside the frame's local port. His knee objected. He shifted his weight until it stopped being relevant and took a compact interface bridge from his bag. Hargreaves's expression changed when he saw it. "Is that certified?" Rook connected the bridge to the service frame. "No." "Should I ask what happens if it fails?" Hargreaves asked. "No," Rook said. Hargreaves's mouth tightened. "Emma Clarke told me you had proper recovery equipment." "This is proper recovery equipment," Rook said. "You just said it isn't certified," Hargreaves replied. Rook looked up at him. "Different question."

He sat against the wall rack and loosened his collar. The interface scars at the base of his skull were pale and ridged. Newer civilian ports were small and polite. His had been designed when the military regarded easy surgical access as a feature. The bridge found the old contacts with a pressure that was not pain until it was. A warning appeared across his vision: UNSUPPORTED FULL EMBODIMENT. NATIVE MOTOR SUPPRESSION UNAVAILABLE. Rook dismissed it. A second warning required an active observer. He looked at Hargreaves. "If I stop breathing, pull that cable." Hargreaves went pale. "You're joking." "Then don't pull it," Rook said. "Mr Rook," Hargreaves said.

Rook authorised the connection. His biological body did not disappear. That was what people expected the first time. They imagined a door closing, darkness, then waking somewhere else. Safe systems could make it feel almost that clean.

Field systems did not bother.

For half a second he had two right hands.

One lay on his thigh, warm, scarred, fingers beginning to curl against denim. The other hung from the service frame's shoulder with no pulse inside it and a tool selector nested where tendons ought to have been. His vision split between human eyes facing a dirty concrete floor and the frame's optical cluster looking down from the rack. Then the body map loaded badly. His stomach attempted to react to a balance system that had no stomach. His right shoulder became forty millimetres wider. His left ankle existed at the wrong angle. The service frame's maintenance software suggested a six-minute calibration sequence. Rook skipped it.

The world settled hard.

He stood in steel.

Behind him, his biological body remained seated against the wall, head tipped slightly forward. Seeing himself from outside had once been disturbing. Then it had become ordinary. Later it became disturbing again for different reasons. The service frame released from the rack. Hargreaves took an involuntary step back. Rook flexed the mechanical hand once. Torque feedback in the left ankle was indeed unreliable. The service log had not exaggerated. He compensated and walked.

The trench hatch resisted until he cut through a seized latch. Inside, the passage was barely wide enough for the service frame's shoulders. Rook moved on hands and knees beneath Bay Four, feeling Malik shift the Briggs through vibration in his mechanical palms. Pressure values became touch because the brain insisted on making a body from whatever it was given.

That part had always been too easy.

He reached the isolation manifold beneath the target bay. The old schematic had been accurate enough. Four hydraulic lines fed the floor clamps and service lifts. A fifth ran to an emergency stabilisation shoe beneath the crane lane. The system existed to prevent damaged heavy frames falling during maintenance. Malik had parked directly over it. Rook opened the manual valve. Above him, something heavy struck concrete. Malik's voice came faintly through the bay structure. "What the hell did you do?" Rook keyed the local speaker. "Made the floor useful."

The stabilisation shoe had risen between the Briggs's feet and locked around the right ankle housing. Not enough to damage the frame. Enough to make walking expensive. Rook started back toward the hatch. Malik pulled. The trench roof groaned. Rook stopped. The Briggs Twelve had enough force to tear the stabiliser out if Malik was willing to destroy the ankle and possibly the bay floor with it. The vibration through Rook's hands increased, then held. He pictured Malik upstairs in the old couch, jaw clenched, the enormous borrowed body telling his nervous system that one foot had become part of the building. "Don't," Rook said through the frame speaker. The pulling stopped. "Get out from under me," Malik answered. "Good idea," Rook said.

He continued crawling. When he climbed out into Bay Three, Hargreaves was no longer there. Rook found him in Bay Four. Too close again. Hargreaves stood six metres in front of the Briggs with both hands visible, attempting to negotiate in the tone of somebody who believed calmness was a force field. "David, the asset is immobilised," he was saying. "Continuing this only exposes you to damage liability. Nobody wants that."

The Briggs leaned against the ankle lock. Rook saw the sequence before Hargreaves did. Malik was not trying to break free. He was shifting weight to the left leg. The left hip was not original. The replacement geometry changed the frame's centre of mass by perhaps three centimetres. Normally irrelevant. Under a forced lateral load, with wet concrete beneath one foot and an ankle fixed to the floor, it mattered. The left foot slipped.

Two point four tonnes began to fall.

Hargreaves froze.

Rook moved.

The service frame was not built to catch a Briggs Twelve. Nothing in the bay was. He hit Hargreaves across the chest with his mechanical forearm and drove him sideways behind the crane column. The impact would bruise him. Better than the alternative. The Briggs came down on one knee hard enough to crack the concrete. Its right arm shot out and caught the floor. The left shoulder struck the crane rail. Metal screamed against metal. Rook's borrowed left ankle lost torque feedback at exactly the wrong moment. The service frame stepped, believed the foot had landed, and discovered it had not. He went down with it. His biological right leg kicked against the wall in Bay Three.

For one ugly instant both bodies demanded priority. Concrete against mechanical shoulder. Human heel against a radiator pipe. An alarm from the interface bridge. Hargreaves shouting something. Malik swearing through the Briggs external speaker. Rook narrowed the world.

Service frame first.

Left hand on concrete. Tool arm clear. Optical horizon six degrees off. Right knee under centre. Stand. The machine obeyed.

His human body became distant noise.

The Briggs remained on one knee, restrained ankle still intact. A dent had appeared in the mismatched left shoulder casing. No fluid leak. No structural failure. Hargreaves was sitting behind the column, eyes wide, one hand against his ribs. Rook walked to the Briggs and stopped outside arm's reach. "Done?" he asked. For several seconds Malik said nothing. Rain hammered the bay roof. Somewhere beyond the fence a freight train passed overhead, its vibration running through the yard after the sound had gone. "That wasn't supposed to happen," Malik said finally. "Usually isn't," Rook said. The massive head unit lowered a fraction. Rook waited. Malik's anger had changed category. That mattered. People could remain angry for hours after they stopped wanting the thing anger had told them to do. "They still owe me," Malik said. "Yes," Rook said.

Hargreaves began to get up. Rook held one mechanical hand toward him without looking back. Stay there. Hargreaves stayed there. "Seventeen years," Malik said. His voice through the speaker had lost some of its amplification as the frame's systems shifted into fault management. "You know what seventeen years in the same body does?" Rook looked at the worn rubber strip somebody had fixed inside the forearm. At the repaired hip. The sticker below the eye. "Enough." Something in the answer reached Malik that argument had not. The Briggs went still. "Don't hard-cut me," Malik said. "Wasn't going to," Rook replied. Rook watched the frame's hands open and rest against the floor. "Give me five minutes," Malik said. The warrant allowed ninety seconds. Rook looked toward Hargreaves. Hargreaves knew what the look meant. "The delay will be recorded." "Record it," Rook said. He turned back to the Briggs. "Five."

Malik did not thank him.

Good.

The frame's head lifted once toward the rain outside the bay. Five minutes later the Briggs sagged. Not dramatically. The posture simply emptied. A machine that had been a man became equipment between one moment and the next. Rook always noticed that moment. He sent the service frame to the wall rack, sat it down and severed the local connection.

Flesh came back all at once.

His first breath was already happening before he remembered lungs. His right heel hurt where it had struck the pipe. The room was lower than it had been, his shoulders narrower, his hands too light. For half a second his left foot expected bad torque feedback and corrected against a fault that did not exist. Rook stayed seated until the floor agreed with his eyes. The interface bridge flashed a yellow neurological warning. He closed it without reading the summary. Hargreaves appeared in the doorway rubbing his ribs. "Are you all right?" he asked. Rook checked his fingers. Five. Tremor minimal. "Yes." Hargreaves looked unconvinced. "You kicked the wall hard enough to dent the radiator." Rook glanced at the radiator. The radiator had been dented before. Probably. "Still yes."

Malik came down from the office ten minutes later in his biological body. He was shorter than Rook had imagined, thick around the middle, with greying hair cut close to the scalp and the unsteady gait of a man whose nervous system still believed each step ought to cover more ground. He kept his right arm slightly away from his body, compensating for a shoulder that was no longer forty centimetres wider. Rook recognised the walk. Malik saw him recognise it. Neither mentioned it.

Hargreaves had opened the formal handover on his tablet. "David, I need your acknowledgement that the frame has been surrendered without continuing dispute as of 09:16. Damage assessment will be separate." Malik looked past him toward Bay Four. "You always this quick after a funeral?" Hargreaves's face tightened. "It's a leased industrial asset." "Chris," Rook said. Hargreaves stopped. Hargreaves was not wrong. Harcourt owned the machine, Malik had known the terms, and seventeen years did not rewrite a contract because a nervous system had learned the machine's left hip. Facts could still arrive at the wrong time. Hargreaves looked down at the handover and changed his tone. "We can complete this digitally later today."

Malik gave Rook a brief, suspicious glance, as though trying to decide whether he had engineered the concession. Rook hadn't. Not exactly. He went to inspect the Briggs. The shoulder damage was cosmetic. The floor impact had cracked an outer knee guard and bent a service cover. The ankle lock had done its job without biting into the actuator housing. Rook released it manually and ran the frame through a low-power diagnostic from the external port. The machine moved badly without Malik.

Factory control returned every joint to nominal response, but nominal was not the body he had watched five minutes earlier. The left hip correction was gone. The right hand closed too symmetrically. None of that meant a person remained inside it. Rook also knew better than to call it nothing. He recorded the damage, tagged the network isolation and authorised Harcourt's transport lock. By 10:03 a recovery carrier had arrived to take the Briggs away. Malik was gone before it loaded. Hargreaves stood beside Rook under the canopy while the carrier's clamps closed around the frame.

"You could have used the disconnect," he said. Rook watched rain gather along the edge of the canopy and fall in a continuous line. "Yes." "Would have saved forty minutes," Hargreaves said. "Probably," Rook replied. Hargreaves looked at him. "You don't like the word probably, do you?" Rook considered the question. "Depends who's saying it."

The carrier lifted the Briggs Twelve clear of the yard. Without Malik's posture, without the blue Arsenal sticker visible from this angle, it looked like what Harcourt's records had always said it was.

Equipment.

Hargreaves signed the completion order. "For what it's worth," he said, "I don't think David's a bad man." Rook zipped his coat. "Didn't ask." Hargreaves gave a tired little laugh. "Emma said you weren't much for conversation." "Emma talks enough for both of us."

That earned a real smile. Rook left him in the rain. Emma called before he reached the station. "You used the field bridge." Rook kept walking. "Morning, Emma," he said. "The interface report uploaded automatically," Emma replied. "Helpful," Rook said. Emma ignored that. "It logged native motor bleed, vestibular conflict and a six-point-seven-second dual-map event." Rook crossed beneath the freightway. Water drummed above him. "Felt shorter." Emma was silent long enough to become dangerous.

"Harcourt is deducting for the shoulder cover," she said at last. "And disputing thirty-eight minutes of labour because they say the forced disconnect was available." "Of course they are," Rook said. "After my commission, tax, interface insurance and the equipment reserve, you've made four hundred and twelve," Emma continued. Rook stepped around a puddle deep enough to contain its own weather system. "Good." "It is not good," Emma said. "It's more than zero," Rook replied. "That philosophy is why you live where you live," Emma said. Rook glanced up at the wet skyline. "I wondered." Emma's expression softened by a degree he pretended not to notice. "Get the bridge checked." "It works," Rook said. "So does a falling lift until it doesn't," Emma replied. "That's not how lifts work," Rook said. "You know what I mean," Emma said. He did.

Rook ended the call at the station entrance. By the time he reached Lower Clapton, the rain had stopped. The clouds remained, bright grey and low, holding the city beneath a light that made every wet surface look newly manufactured. Jaz was outside Rook's building again. This time he was standing over a young food-delivery rider whose box lay open on the pavement. Jaz had one hand on the bike and was explaining, with increasing volume, that the rider had clipped him around the corner. There was no mark on Jaz. Kelly stood beside the entrance smoking something through a short glass inhaler, enjoying the performance.

"You nearly broke my fucking leg," Jaz told the rider. "I didn't touch you," the rider said. Jaz tightened his grip on the bike. "Calling me a liar now?" Rook approached from behind him. Kelly saw him first. Her expression changed. Not fear exactly. Calculation becoming less optimistic. "Tom," she said. Jaz released the bike before turning around. "All sorted," he announced. The rider stared at him. Rook looked at the meal packs on the wet pavement. One had split at the corner. "Looks sorted," he said. Jaz spread his hands. "Misunderstanding." The rider bent to pick up the food. His hands were shaking. Rook crouched, retrieved the unbroken pack and put it back in the box. "Go," he told him. The rider did not need telling twice. Jaz watched the bike disappear toward the junction. "Didn't have to make me look like a prick." Rook straightened. His knee hurt now exactly as promised. "I wasn't involved in that part." Kelly barked a laugh through smoke. Jaz's face hardened, then loosened again when he remembered who he was looking at. "Still got that printer," he said.

Rook went inside. The lift remained dead, so he took the stairs to the fourth floor. On the third landing his right leg trembled once under load. He stopped, not because he needed to, but because falling down municipal stairs after successfully recovering a two-point-four-tonne industrial Frame would have been embarrassing in a way death sometimes wasn't. At his door he found a new dent beside the lock. Kelly, perhaps. Jaz, perhaps. The building, possibly. Nothing important had changed. Inside, he hung the wet coat over the shower rail and stripped the interface bridge from his bag.

The skin at the base of his skull was red around two contacts. He cleaned them in the bathroom mirror. The man looking back appeared younger than the scars suggested and more tired than the face had earned. Time no longer had the courtesy to make one argument at a time. Rook touched the swollen edge of one port. The field bridge diagnostic pulsed amber on the counter.

NATIVE MOTOR BLEED DETECTED.
MEDICAL REVIEW ADVISED.

He dismissed the notice. Through the wall, somebody's television murmured. Pipes moved water somewhere above him. A train passed far enough away to become vibration before sound. Rook heated a meal he had bought two days earlier and stood at the window while it cooked. London stretched away under clearing cloud. Sunlight caught the higher buildings while water still shone in the older streets. Beside the canal, a municipal frame lifted a blocked grate. Somewhere, its operator would feel the weight through hands that were not hands and perhaps never think about the distinction. Rook's payment arrived. £412.18, less a pending hold against damage review. He looked at the number until the display dimmed.

Four hundred and twelve pounds for getting a man out of a body he did not own, returning the machine to people who did, bruising a leasing representative and adding another yellow flag to his medical record. Useful work. The phrase came without irony, which irritated him. The meal unit chimed. Rook ate at the narrow counter because the table was covered in tools. When he finished, he washed the fork, checked tomorrow's weather and opened the recovery board.

There were seven jobs within London. Two had already been taken. One paid badly enough to constitute an insult. Another involved domestic property and an active family dispute, which usually meant three hours in a hallway while people explained why the law should apply differently to them. Rook selected none of them. Outside, Kelly started shouting again. He listened until he was satisfied nobody was being hit, then made another coffee.

Tomorrow there would be something else to recover.

End of Chapter One
